# Study Guide: Community-Acquired Pneumonia (NAC) for Medical Residency Exams

## 1. Introduction and Classification
Community-Acquired Pneumonia (NAC) is defined as an infection of the lung parenchyma. For clinical evaluation and exam purposes, pneumonia is categorized into four distinct groups:
*   Community-Acquired Pneumonia (NAC).
*   Hospital-acquired pneumonia.
*   Ventilator-associated pneumonia.
*   Healthcare-associated pneumonia (including NAC caused by resistant pathogens).

### Risk Factors for Resistant Pathogens
Broad-spectrum coverage should not be applied indiscriminately. Therapy must be tailored based on specific risk profiles.

| Category | Clinical Risk Factors |
| :--- | :--- |
| **MDR Gram-Negative & MRSA** | Hospitalization $\geq$ 48h in the last 3 months; Antibiotic use in the last 90 days; Immunosuppression; Chronic hemodialysis (last session within 30 days); Non-ambulatory status; Tube feeding; Gastric acid suppression; Severe COPD or bronchiectasis (*Pseudomonas*). |
| **Nosocomial MRSA** | Hospitalization $\geq$ 48h in the last 3 months; Antibiotic use in the last 90 days; Neutropenia; Documented previous MRSA colonization; Heart failure; Concurrent Influenza; Gastric acid suppression. |
| **Community-Acquired MRSA** | Cavitary infiltrates or necrosis; Abundant hemoptysis; Erythema; Young and previously healthy status; Presentation during summer months. |

> **Clinical Rule: The "Threshold of Resistance"**
> Academic evidence suggests that at least **2-3 risk factors** are generally required to justify the initiation of broad-spectrum empiric therapy. Over-treating patients with a single risk factor (e.g., gastric acid suppression alone) is a common clinical and exam-room error.

## 2. Epidemiology and Risk Profiles
Incidence peaks in the elderly and young children during winter months. Approximately 20% of NAC cases require hospitalization.

**Epidemiological Trend:** While *Streptococcus pneumoniae* remains the most common pathogen, its incidence is **decreasing** due to the widespread implementation of pneumococcal vaccination.

### Categorized Risk Factors by Pathogen
*   **General Pneumonia:** Alcoholism, asthma, immunosuppression, recent hospitalization, and age > 70 years.
*   **Pneumococcal Pneumonia (*S. pneumoniae*):** Senile dementia, seizures, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, smoking, alcoholism, COPD, and HIV infection.
*   **Gram-negative Bacilli (Enterobacteriaceae) & *P. aeruginosa*:** Recent hospitalization, previous antimicrobial treatment, alcoholism, and heart or renal failure. *P. aeruginosa* is specifically associated with structural lung disease (bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, or severe COPD).
*   **Legionella (Legionnaire's Disease):** Diabetes, hematological malignancies, cancer, severe nephropathy, HIV infection, smoking, male gender, and recent stay in a hotel or on a cruise ship.
*   **Anaerobic Infection:** Requires the combination of "defenseless airways" (alcohol, drugs, or seizures) and significant gingivitis.
    *   **High-Yield Note:** Anaerobes typically intervene **days or weeks** after the initial aspiration event and are frequently associated with complications such as lung abscesses or empyema.

## 3. Microbial Etiology by Site of Care
The likely pathogen is dictated by the severity of the presentation and the setting of care.

| Site of Care | Common Pathogens |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Ambulatory (Outpatient)** | *S. pneumoniae*, *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Chlamydia pneumoniae*, Respiratory viruses. |
| **General Ward (Inpatient)** | *S. pneumoniae*, *M. pneumoniae*, *C. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, *Legionella* species, Respiratory viruses. |
| **Intensive Care Unit (ICU)** | *S. pneumoniae*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Legionella* species, Gram-negative bacilli, *H. influenzae*, Respiratory viruses. |

> **Clinical Pearl: "Atypical" Pathogens**
> Pathogens such as ***M. pneumoniae*, *C. pneumoniae*, and *Legionella*** are **intrinsically resistant to all beta-lactams**. When these are suspected, treatment must include a macrolide, a fluoroquinolone, or a tetracycline. Note that 10-15% of NAC cases are polimicrobial (typical + atypical).

## 4. Clinical Presentation and Complications
### Classic Symptoms and Signs
*   **Symptoms:** Fever, productive cough (rusty or purulent sputum), pleuritic pain, chills, and dyspnea.
*   **Systemic Symptoms:** Headache, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, myalgias, arthralgias, and fatigue.
*   **Physical Exam:** Tachypnea, dullness to percussion, increased tactile/vocal fremitus, crepitant rales (crackles), and pleural rub.
*   **Elderly Presentation:** Often atypical; symptoms may be limited to **falls or new-onset confusion**.

### Cardiovascular Complications
Systemic inflammation and procoagulant activity in NAC significantly elevate cardiac risk:
*   **Acute Coronary Syndromes:** Up to 90% occur within the **first week** of onset.
*   **Heart Failure:** The risk of new-onset congestive heart failure in hospitalized elderly patients remains elevated for up to **one year**.

## 5. Diagnostic Strategy
*   **Imaging:** **Chest X-ray** is required to differentiate NAC from other pathologies. **Computed Tomography (TAC)** is rarely necessary, reserved specifically for suspected **post-obstructive pneumonia** (tumor or foreign body).
*   **Microbiology:** Blood cultures (hemocultivos) have low rescue rates (5-14%). They are indicated only for **high-risk patients**: neutropenia secondary to pneumonia, asplenia, complement deficiency, chronic liver disease, or severe pneumonia.
*   **Antigen & Serology:** Urinary antigens for *Legionella* (**serotype 1**) and *Pneumococcus* (70% sensitivity) are high-yield. Serology is no longer preferred because it requires convalescent samples, delaying acute management.
*   **PCR & Biomarkers:** PCR of nasopharyngeal swabs is the gold standard for viral diagnosis, specifically for **Influenza, Parainfluenza, and RSV**.
    *   **CRP:** Useful for detecting therapeutic failure or aggravation.
    *   **Procalcitonin:** Guides the necessity of initiating or continuing antibiotics.

## 6. Severity Assessment and Triage (The "Exam Essentials")
### Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI)
*   **Classes I-II:** Ambulatory treatment.
*   **Class III:** Observation unit or brief hospitalization.
*   **Classes IV-V:** Mandatory hospital admission.

### CURB-65 Score Criteria
One point is assigned for each of the following:

| Letter | Definition | Criteria |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **C** | Confusion | New onset mental confusion |
| **U** | Urea | $> 7$ mmol/L |
| **R** | Respiratory Rate | $> 30$ breaths per minute |
| **B** | Blood Pressure | Systolic $< 90$ mmHg OR Diastolic $< 60$ mmHg |
| **65** | Age | $\geq 65$ years |

### Triage Decision
*   **0 Points:** Ambulatory treatment.
*   **1–2 Points:** Hospital admission (unless the score is solely due to age $\geq 65$ and the patient is stable).
*   **3+ Points:** Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission.

### Factors Suggesting Early Deterioration
Even with low severity scores, monitor for these high-risk indicators:
*   **Clinical:** Multilobular infiltrates, severe hypoxemia (SatO2 $< 90\%$), confusion, taquipnea ($> 30$/min).
*   **Laboratory:** Hypoalbuminemia, neutropenia, severe acidosis (pH $< 7.30$), thrombocytopenia, hyponatremia, and hypoglycemia.

## 7. Empiric Antibiotic Therapy
The most significant risk factor for antibiotic-resistant pneumococcal infection is the use of **any antibiotic within the previous 3 months**.

### Initial Empiric Antibiotic Regimens
| Patient Profile | Recommended Regimen (Options) | Route |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Healthy Outpatient** (No antibiotics in last 90 days) | **Macrolides:** Clarithromycin (500mg every 12h) OR Azithromycin (500mg day 1, then 250mg/day). <br>**Alternative:** Doxycycline (100mg every 12h). | Oral (VO) |
| **Outpatient with Comorbidities** (or recent antibiotic use) | **Respiratory Quinolone:** Levofloxacin (750mg/d), Moxifloxacin (400mg/d), or Gemifloxacin (320mg/d). <br>**OR Beta-lactam:** (Amoxicillin 1g every 8h, Ceftriaxone 1-2g/d, Cefpodoxime 200mg every 12h, or Cefuroxima 500mg every 12h) **PLUS a Macrolide.** | Oral (VO) |
| **Inpatient (General Ward)** | **Respiratory Quinolone:** Levofloxacin (750mg/d) or Moxifloxacin (400mg/d). <br>**OR Beta-lactam:** (Cefotaxime 1-2g every 8h, Ceftriaxone 1-2g/d, Ampicillin 1-2g every 4-6h, or Ertapenem 1g/d) **PLUS a Macrolide.** | IV or VO |

**Warning:** If macrolide resistance in *S. pneumoniae* exceeds 25% (as in the US), **macrolides must not be used as monotherapy.**

## 8. Management Checklist: Steps for Successful NAC Treatment
1.  **Assess Severity:** Check vitals and saturation. You must manually count the respiratory rate for **one full minute**.
2.  **Stabilize:** Ensure adequate oxygenation and circulatory support.
3.  **Investigate Etiology:** Order cultures and antigens for high-risk or severe cases.
4.  **Determine Site of Care:** Utilize CURB-65/PSI alongside clinical and psychosocial judgment.
5.  **Initiate Empiric Therapy:** Promptly start antibiotics based on the risk of resistance (the "3-month rule").
6.  **Discard Empyema:** Perform thoracentesis if a pleural effusion $> 1$ cm is detected on a lateral decubitus X-ray.
