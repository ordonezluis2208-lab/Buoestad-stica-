# Gastroenterology Study Guide: Chronic Diarrhea, Malabsorption, and Irritable Bowel Syndrome

This study guide provides a comprehensive overview of the clinical definitions, diagnostic criteria, and management strategies for chronic diarrhea, malabsorption syndromes, and Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS), based on clinical gastroenterology data.

---

## I. Key Concepts and Clinical Foundations

### Chronic Diarrhea: Definition and Classifications
Diarrhea is defined as the expulsion of unformed or abnormally liquid stools with increased frequency. Quantitatively, it is characterized by fecal weight or water exceeding 200 to 225 ml/g in 24 hours. **Chronic diarrhea** is specifically defined by a duration of more than four weeks.

#### Pathophysiological Mechanisms
The classification of chronic diarrhea is based on the underlying mechanism:

| Mechanism | Key Characteristics | Key Findings |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Secretory** | Persistent despite fasting; voluminous, watery, and generally painless. | Normal fecal osmotic gap (<25); often caused by medications (laxatives), hormones (VIPoma, gastrinoma), or infections. |
| **Osmotic** | Disappears with fasting or cessation of the offending substance. | Fecal osmotic gap >50; caused by malabsorption of nutrients (lactose) or ingestion of non-absorbable carbohydrates (sorbitol). |
| **Steatorreic (Malabsorptive)** | Pale, pasty, or watery stools with a rancid odor; adherent to the bowl. | Fecal fat excretion >7 g/day; causes include pancreatic insufficiency or small bowel disease (Celiac). |
| **Inflammatory** | Accompanied by pain, fever, and blood; presence of fecal leukocytes or calprotectin. | Associated with IBD, infections, or colorectal cancer in older adults. |
| **Dysmotility** | Altered transit time. | Seen in IBS, hyperthyroidism, or diabetic diarrhea. |
| **Factitious (Simulated)** | Secret self-administration of laxatives or Munchausen syndrome. | More common in women; often presents with hypotension and hypokalemia. |

#### Alarm Signs in Chronic Diarrhea
Clinical indicators that suggest organic disease rather than functional disorders include:
*   Duration of symptoms less than three months.
*   Nocturnal diarrhea or pain that awakens the patient.
*   Visible or occult digestive bleeding (bloody stools).
*   Unexplained weight loss or fever.
*   Anemia.
*   Greasy or high-volume stools.

### Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)
IBS is a functional digestive disorder characterized by altered bowel habits and abdominal pain in the absence of detectable structural abnormalities.

#### Rome IV Diagnostic Criteria
To be diagnosed with IBS, a patient must experience recurrent abdominal pain, on average at least one day per week during the last three months, associated with two or more of the following:
1.  Relation to defecation.
2.  Association with a change in the frequency of stool.
3.  Association with a change in the form (appearance) of stool.
*Note: Symptoms must have started at least six months prior to diagnosis.*

#### Clinical Spectrum and Treatment
IBS is classified by severity (Leve/70%, Moderado/25%, Grave/5%). Management is symptom-based:
*   **Diarrhea:** Loperamide, Cholestyramine.
*   **Constipation:** Insoluble fiber (Psyllium), Osmotic laxatives (PEG), Linaclotide.
*   **Abdominal Pain:** Smooth muscle relaxants, Tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs.
*   **Gas/Bloating:** Low FODMAP diet, Probiotics, Rifaximin.

---

### Malabsorption Syndromes
Malabsorption involves defects in the digestion and absorption of nutrients. While most lead to steatorrhea, exceptions include primary lactase deficiency and pernicious anemia.

#### Specific Diagnostic Tests
*   **Sudan III Stain:** Qualitative screening for fecal fat.
*   **Quantitative Fecal Fat:** Normal is <7 g/day.
*   **D-Xylose Test:** Assesses proximal small bowel mucosa function. Normal excretion is >4.5 g in 5 hours; low levels suggest duodenal/jejunal disease.
*   **Schilling Test:** Determines the etiology of Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) deficiency (e.g., Pernicious Anemia vs. Pancreatitis vs. Bacterial Overgrowth).

## II. Short-Answer Practice Questions

1.  **What is the quantitative definition of diarrhea?**
    *   *Answer:* An increase in fecal water or weight exceeding 200 to 225 ml/g in a 24-hour period.
2.  **How is the fecal osmotic gap calculated, and what is the normal osmolarity?**
    *   *Answer:* Calculated as 2 x (Fecal $Na^+$ + Fecal $K^+$) subtracted from total fecal osmolarity. Normal osmolarity is 300 mosmol/kg $H_20$.
3.  **What distinguishes secretory diarrhea from osmotic diarrhea regarding fasting?**
    *   *Answer:* Secretory diarrhea persists despite fasting, whereas osmotic diarrhea disappears when the patient stops ingesting the poorly absorbed substance.
4.  **Which microorganism causes Whipple's Disease, and what is the hallmark biopsy finding?**
    *   *Answer:* *Tropheryma whipplei*. The hallmark finding is PAS-positive macrophages in the lamina propria.
5.  **What are the fecal fat thresholds for Pancreatic Exocrine Insufficiency vs. Small Intestine Disease?**
    *   *Answer:* Small intestine disease typically averages 15–25 g/day, while Pancreatic Exocrine Insufficiency often exceeds 32 g/day.
6.  **What is the "Pancreatic Cholera" syndrome?**
    *   *Answer:* Also known as VIPoma, it is a non-beta cell pancreatic adenoma secreting VIP, leading to massive watery diarrhea (>3L/day), hypokalemia, and achlorhydria.
7.  **Identify three risk factors for Post-Infectious IBS.**
    *   *Answer:* Long duration of initial illness, toxicity of the infecting strain (e.g., Campylobacter, Salmonella), female gender, smoking, and psychological factors like depression or anxiety.
8.  **What is the standard treatment for Tropical Sprue?**
    *   *Answer:* Tetracyclines for up to six months and Folic Acid supplementation.
9.  **Which diagnostic test is used specifically to evaluate the exocrine function of the pancreas?**
    *   *Answer:* The Secretin Test (intravenous administration followed by duodenal intubation to collect secretions).
10. **What does a low fecal pH indicate?**
    *   *Answer:* It suggests carbohydrate malabsorption (e.g., lactose intolerance).

## IV. Glossary of Important Terms

*   **Achlorhydria:** The absence of hydrochloric acid in the gastric secretions.
*   **Calprotectin:** A leukocyte protein found in feces; its presence indicates intestinal inflammation.
*   **Dermatitis Herpetiforme:** A skin manifestation (intense itching and blisters) frequently associated with Celiac Disease.
*   **EII (Enfermedad Inflamatoria Intestinal):** Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD).
*   **FODMAP:** Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides, and Polyols; a group of carbohydrates that can trigger IBS symptoms.
*   **Hypocratismo Digital:** Also known as "clubbing"; swelling of the fingertips associated with malabsorption or IBD.
*   **PAS+ Macrophages:** Macrophages that stain positive with Periodic Acid-Schiff; a diagnostic indicator for Whipple's Disease.
*   **SBCID (Sobrecrecimiento Bacteriano del Intestino Delgado):** Small Bowel Bacterial Overgrowth (SIBO).
*   **Steatorrhea:** The presence of excess fat in feces, indicating malabsorption.
*   **Tenesmo:** A continual or recurrent inclination to evacuate the bowels, often associated with IBS or inflammation.
*   **VEDA (Videoendoscopia Digestiva Alta):** Upper Gastrointestinal Endoscopy.
*   **VIPoma:** A rare tumor that secretes Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide (VIP), leading to severe watery diarrhea.
