# Neurology Residency Exam Guide: Comprehensive Study of Cephalalgias

## 1. Anatomical and Physiological Foundations of Headache

Pain sensation in the cranial region results from the stimulation of peripheral nociceptors due to tissue lesions, visceral distension, or the direct activation/lesion of pain pathways within the peripheral or central nervous systems.

### Pain Sensitivity of Cranial Structures
| Pain-Sensitive Structures | Insensitive to Pain |
| :--- | :--- |
| Scalp | Ependyma |
| Middle meningeal artery | Choroid plexus |
| Dural sinuses | Pial veins |
| **Hoz (Falx cerebri)** | **Most of** the brain parenchyma ("gran parte") |
| Proximal segments of large pial arteries | |

### Systems Involved in Headache Pathophysiology
*   **Peripheral Nociception:** Involves the large intracranial vessels, the dura mater, and the peripheral nerve endings of the Trigeminal nerve (V cranial nerve).
*   **Trigeminocervical System:** Central processing of pain involving the ventroposteromedial thalamus and the cortex.
*   **Pain Modulating Systems:** Encephalic structures that receive impulses from trigeminal nociceptors to regulate and modulate pain perception.

**The Trigeminovascular System**
This system consists of the innervation of the dura mater and large cerebral vessels by the Trigeminal nerve. Its activation reflects the stimulation of cranial parasympathetic pathways. 
*   **High-Yield Clinical Concept:** Migraine and other primary headaches are **not vascular headaches**. Pain is driven by the activation of autonomous systems and the release of vasoactive substances (e.g., CGRP), not by simple vascular changes.

## 2. Clinical Evaluation and Warning Signs (Red Flags)

Differentiating between primary intrinsic disorders and secondary headaches caused by exogenous, potentially lethal conditions is the first priority of clinical evaluation.

### High-Priority Clinical Checklist (Red Flags)
*   **Sudden onset** or "explosive" pain (Thunderclap).
*   **First intense headache** ever experienced.
*   **New onset after age 55.**
*   **Subacute headache that worsens over days or weeks.**
*   **Anomalies on neurological examination.**
*   **Vomiting preceding the headache** (characteristic of posterior fossa tumors).
*   Pain induced by **bending at the waist (flexionar la cintura)**, coughing, lifting weight, or physical effort (suggests posterior fossa tumor or Chiari malformation).
*   Pain that interrupts sleep or appears immediately upon waking (**Note:** only 10% of tumor-related headaches actually interrupt sleep).
*   Fever or unexplained systemic signs.
*   Pain linked to local tenderness (e.g., temporal artery region).

### Serious Secondary Causes Requiring Immediate Surveillance
*   Meningitis
*   Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (HSA)
*   Epidural Hematoma (HED)
*   Subdural Hematoma (HSD)
*   Glaucoma
*   Purulent Sinusitis

### Migraine Disability Assessment Score (MIDAS)
Assesses disability over the last 90 days. Residents must be familiar with the **>20 point threshold** for Grade IV.
1.  **Grade I (0–5 points):** Minimal or infrequent disability.
2.  **Grade II (6–10 points):** Mild or infrequent disability.
3.  **Grade III (11–20 points):** Moderate disability.
4.  **Grade IV (>20 points):** Intense disability.

## 3. Secondary Headaches: High-Yield Clinical Features

*   **Meningitis:** Often mimics migraine with pulsatile pain, photophobia, and vomiting. **Lumbar puncture** is essential. Pain is frequently exacerbated by eye movement.
*   **Intracranial Hemorrhage:** May present as isolated headache. CT scans may appear normal if the bleed is very small or located below the foramen magnum.
*   **Brain Tumors:** Present in 30% of cases. Pain is typically deep, sorda (dull), and **intermittent** in early stages. **Vomiting that precedes the headache** by weeks is a hallmark of posterior fossa tumors.
*   **Temporal Arteritis (Giant Cell Arteritis):** Affects patients around **70 years old (65% women)**. It is the **leading cause of rapid-onset bilateral blindness in patients >60 years**.
    *   **Clinical Clues:** Jaw claudication, scalp tenderness (pain when combing hair), **nodules or red streaks** over the temporal arteries, fever, and weight loss.
    *   **Prognosis:** **50% of untreated patients go blind**; corticosteroid treatment is mandatory.
    *   **Note:** Migrainous patients may also respond to prednisone; interpret treatment response cautiously.
*   **Glaucoma:** Postrating headache with **intense ocular pain**. Signs: red eye, **fixed and moderately dilated pupil**, nausea, and vomiting.

## 4. Primary Headache Comparison: Migraine, Tension, and Cluster

| Feature | Tension-Type Headache | Migraine | Cluster (Racimo) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Epidemiology** | 69% prevalence. | 15% Women, 6% Men. | 0.1% of adults. Male:Female 3:1. |
| **Pathophysiology** | **Muscle contraction** is a distinguishing sign. | Trigeminovascular activation; CGRP release; dopamine involvement. | Central hypothalamic "pacemaker" disorder; sympathetic deficit. |
| **Location** | Bilateral ("in a band"). | Unilateral (typically). | Unilateral (orbital/retro-orbital). |
| **Quality/Duration** | Gradual, dull; lasts days. | Pulsatile; 4–72 hours. | Penetrating, explosive; 15–180 min. |
| **Intercritical Sign**| N/A | Variable | Patients **feel well** between attacks. |
| **Differential** | Generally no nausea/vomiting. | **Syncope (10%)**, **Seizures (4%)**, **Fosfenos** (flashes). | **Lack of response to Indomethacin**. |

**Migraine Aura (20–25%)**
Involves visual phenomena such as **Teichopsia** (zigzag lines) or **Fosfenos** (flashes) moving across the field.

## 6. Evidence-Based Therapeutic Management

### Acute Migraine Treatment
*   **NSAIDs:** First-line for mild/moderate. Naproxen (220–550mg), Ibuprofen (400mg), Diclofenac (50mg).
*   **5-HT1 Agonists (Triptans):** Rizatriptan is fastest. **Almotriptan, Frovatriptan, and Naratriptan** have slower onset but **better tolerance**.
*   **Ergotamines:** Dose must be **non-nauseating (2mg)**. Often combined with **Caffeine (100mg)** to potentiate the vasoconstrictor effect.
*   **Synergy:** Aspirin + Metoclopramide is equivalent to oral Sumatriptan.

### Prophylactic Migraine Treatment
*   **Criteria:** Start if **≥ 4 attacks per month**.
*   **First-line:** Amitriptyline, Propranolol, Topiramate, and Valproate. Maintain for 6 months after stabilization.

### Cluster Headache (Cefalea en Racimos) Protocol
*   **Acute:** 100% Oxygen (10–12 L/min) and Sumatriptan 6mg SC.
*   **Prophylaxis:**
    *   **Short-term:** Prednisone (1mg/kg) or **Arnold's Greater Occipital Nerve Injection**.
    *   **Long-term:** **Verapamil** (first-line); Lithium for chronic.

> ### **Clinical Pearl: Verapamil Monitoring**
> Doses range from 160–960 mg/day. AV Block can occur **even with doses of 240 mg/d**. Mandatory baseline ECG is required, repeated 10 days after dose increases and every 6 months during long-term use.

## 7. Specific Contraindications and Clinical Notes

**Warning: Triptan Usage**
*   **Contraindications:** Strictly avoided in patients with a history of **stroke (ACV)** or **seizures**.
*   **Timing:** Triptans are **ineffective** if administered during the aura; they must be taken **after the aura is complete** and the headache has begun.

**Route of Administration and Efficacy**
Oral Sumatriptan is **ineffective** for acute cluster. 
*   **Parenteral DHE Timings:** Plasma concentration reached in **3 min (EV)**, **30 min (IM)**, or **45 min (SC)**. 
*   **Nasal Sprays:** Take **30–60 minutes** to reach concentration and have only 50-60% efficacy compared to the 80-90% relief seen with parenteral routes.
